Your pay stub explained: where your money goes before payday

Updated November 18, 2026 · 6 min read · For people in Canada

In short: Gross pay minus income tax (federal and provincial), CPP and EI, plus any benefits or dues, equals net pay. Check your hours, keep your TD1 up to date, and budget on net, never gross.

If your paycheque always feels smaller than it should, you're not imagining it. Here's what each line on a typical Canadian pay stub means, using an example worked out with MapleBook's paycheque estimator. Your numbers will be different.

Income tax: two kinds

In Canada you pay federal and provincial (or territorial) income tax, and your employer withholds both from each paycheque. How much they take off depends partly on the TD1 forms you filled in when you started. If your life changed, like a second job, a new child or a move to another province, it may be worth updating them.

CPP and EI

CPP contributions build toward your own Canada Pension Plan benefit. EI premiums fund Employment Insurance, which can help if you lose your job or take parental leave. Both have yearly maximums, so higher earners can hit the cap before December and see bigger paycheques for the rest of the year.

The lines people skip

  • Year to date (YTD): totals since January, handy for checking you're on track.
  • Vacation pay, benefits, RRSP matching or union dues may also appear.

Three pay stub mistakes worth checking

  • Your hours or rate are wrong. Compare against your own record every time.
  • Your TD1 is out of date after a life change.
  • Two jobs that both claim the basic personal amount, which can mean too little tax withheld and a bill in April.

If a line makes no sense, ask payroll first. To estimate your own take-home pay, use the take-home pay calculator.

Common questions

Why is my net pay so much lower than my gross pay?

Income tax (federal and provincial), CPP contributions, EI premiums and any benefits or dues are taken off before you are paid. The amount left is your net pay.

Do CPP and EI come off every paycheque?

Generally yes, until you reach the yearly maximum. After that, those deductions stop for the rest of the calendar year.
